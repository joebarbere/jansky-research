\title{Survey Flux Calibration Limits a Two-Beam FASHI--ALFALFA Test for H\,I Beam Blending}
\author[0009-0008-3289-4447]{Joseph Barbere}
\affiliation{Independent researcher}

\begin{abstract}
Beam blending should raise a galaxy's H\,I flux more in the wider Arecibo beam
(\hbRealBeamAlfa~arcmin) than in the FAST beam (\hbRealBeamFast~arcmin). We tested this on
\hbRealNTargets\ galaxies matched between FASHI DR2 and ALFALFA $\alpha$.100, with controls
fixed in advance. Power, injection and chance-match controls passed, but
neighbours too far apart in velocity to blend gave a slope of
$\hbRealBetaNull\pm\hbRealBetaNullErr$ instead of zero, so the test was inconclusive. The
flux calibration misfits held-out isolated galaxies by up to \hbRealDzeroTop~dex, depending on
signal-to-noise. A second, pre-specified attempt with a more flexible calibration cut the misfit to \hbRealVtwoMaxA--\hbRealVtwoMaxB~dex but
still failed its held-out test, so no blending slope is quoted. That is comparable to the
\hbRealSignal~dex signal of one equal-flux neighbour.
\end{abstract}

\section{Test}
\label{sec:test}
Confusion in H\,I surveys has been estimated by simulation \citep{jones2015}, but not, so far as
we know, measured on the same galaxies with two beams. FASHI DR2 \citep{fashi_dr2} gives a FAST
beam of \hbRealBeamFast~arcmin at 1420~MHz (its Table~1), and \citet{giovanelli2005} give an ALFA beam
averaging \hbRealBeamAlfa~arcmin (their pp.~8 and 22; the beam is elliptical, 3.3 by 3.8~arcmin). We matched
FASHI DR2 (\hbRealNFashi\ sources) to ALFALFA $\alpha$.100 code-1 detections
\citep[\hbRealNAlfalfa\ sources in all;][]{haynes2018} within 1.5~arcmin and 100~km~s$^{-1}$.
Shifting one catalogue by 10~arcmin gives a chance-match rate of \hbRealChance\%.

For a target of flux $S_c$ with neighbours $n$ at separation $s_n$, a Gaussian beam of width
$\theta$ adds $S_n\exp(-4\ln2\,s_n^2/\theta^2)$ when the two profiles overlap in velocity. The
predicted log ratio $R$ of the ALFA and FAST fluxes is largest for neighbours about 2~arcmin
away; it reaches \hbRealSignal~dex for one equal-flux neighbour. The statistic is the slope
$\beta$ of the observed log flux ratio on $R$ after the survey-to-survey flux scale has been
removed. Blending predicts $\beta\approx1$; extra gas in close pairs, which both surveys would
see, predicts $\beta\approx0$. The flux scale (a function of signal-to-noise, line width, flux
and declination) was fitted only on \hbRealNIsolated\ isolated targets, with no neighbour
within 6~arcmin, and applied unchanged to the \hbRealNPrimary\ targets with a neighbour at
1--6~arcmin in the same velocity window. The negative control uses \hbRealNNull\ targets with
neighbours at the same separations but more than 600~km~s$^{-1}$ away in velocity, which cannot
blend; its slope must be within $2\sigma$ of zero. Errors come from a bootstrap over groups of
targets linked within 6~arcmin.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
On simulated fluxes with the real geometry and errors, a planted $\beta=1$ was detected at
$3\sigma$ in \hbRealPowerFrac\% of trials. Blends injected into real isolated targets returned
$\beta=\hbRealInjOne\pm\hbRealInjOneSd$ (mean and scatter of ten injections), and $\hbRealInjZero$ when nothing was injected. The
negative control failed: $\beta_{\rm null}=\hbRealBetaNull\pm\hbRealBetaNullErr$, against
$\beta=\hbRealBeta\pm\hbRealBetaErr$ for the primary sample. By the rule fixed in advance, the
outcome is inconclusive.

Diagnostics were then run to locate the failure, with their expected outcomes recorded before
they were run. With the calibration fitted on a random half of the isolated targets, the other
half's median residual reaches \hbRealDzeroTop~dex ($\hbRealDzeroTopSig\sigma$) in the
highest signal-to-noise quintile and \hbRealDzeroMid~dex in the second-lowest
(Figure~\ref{fig:heldout}). Weighted to the primary sample's signal-to-noise, the null slope
falls to $\hbRealMatchedNull\pm\hbRealMatchedNullErr$; it is $\hbRealTercLo\pm\hbRealTercLoErr$
in the faintest third of the null sample and $\hbRealTercHi\pm\hbRealTercHiErr$ in the
brightest, a difference of under $2\sigma$. Local galaxy density does not account for it
(coefficient $\hbRealDensity\pm\hbRealDensityErr$).

A second attempt, specified before it was run, replaced the quadratic signal-to-noise term with
a linear spline with knots at the deciles, added local density, and put a held-out test first.
The calibration was fitted on isolated targets in alternate 2-degree strips of right ascension
and tested on the others, in both directions. Every decile had to lie within 0.010~dex of zero,
with $p>0.01$ for the ten together. The largest decile residual fell from
\hbRealVoneMax~dex for the original form to \hbRealVtwoMaxA\ and \hbRealVtwoMaxB~dex, but both
directions failed ($p=\hbRealVtwoPA$ and $\hbRealVtwoPB$). What remains is at the two ends of
the signal-to-noise range: \hbRealVtwoLoMin\ to \hbRealVtwoLoMax~dex in the faintest decile and
$+\hbRealVtwoHiMin$ to $+\hbRealVtwoHiMax$~dex in the brightest
($\hbRealVtwoSigMin$--$\hbRealVtwoSigMax\sigma$). The run therefore stopped before the
blending slope was measured.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
The FASHI and ALFALFA flux scales differ in a way that depends on signal-to-noise, as the FASHI
DR2 comparison already showed for faint sources \citep[][\S~5.2]{fashi_dr2}. Our calibrations,
fitted on isolated galaxies, leave held-out residuals of up to \hbRealVtwoMaxA--\hbRealVtwoMaxB~dex
at the ends of the signal-to-noise range. That is comparable to the blending signal, and the failed negative
control is at least partly a calibration effect. This catalogue comparison therefore cannot
separate beam blending from a real H\,I excess in close pairs. A calibration that varies with
sky position, or a test restricted to the signal-to-noise range where the calibration holds,
would each need its own control fixed in advance. Interferometric H\,I imaging of the pairs
would settle the question directly.
