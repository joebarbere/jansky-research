\title{Recovering the Flat Inner Milky Way Rotation Curve from Public LAB HI~21\,cm Data
by the Tangent-Point Method}

\author[0009-0008-3289-4447]{Joseph Barbere}
\affiliation{Independent researcher}

\begin{abstract}
The flat rotation curve of the Milky Way is a foundational piece of evidence for dark matter, and the
classical way to measure its inner part is the HI~21\,cm tangent-point method. We ask whether that
textbook result can be recovered from public survey data with a small, tested, CPU-only tool. We read
\hiNlong\ Galactic-longitude $(b,v)$ slices from the Leiden/Argentine/Bonn (LAB) HI survey
\citep{kalberla2005}, extract the terminal velocity of each $b=0$ profile with two estimators run on
the same spectra --- a fixed 2~K brightness-temperature threshold and an error-function fit to the
profile edge --- and apply $R = R_0\sin\ell$, $V(R) = v_\mathrm{term} + V_0\sin\ell$ with
$R_0=\hiRzero$~kpc and $V_0=\hiVzero$~km\,s$^{-1}$ \citep{reid2019}. Beyond the bar-dominated inner
region, over the \hiRealNflat\ points from $R\approx\hiRealRmin$ to $\hiRealRmax$~kpc, the rotation
speed holds at $V=\hiRealVflat$~km\,s$^{-1}$ (point-to-point scatter \hiRealVflatScatter, standard
error of the mean \hiRealVflatSem) with a fitted slope of
$\hiRealSlope\pm\hiRealSlopeErr$~km\,s$^{-1}$\,kpc$^{-1}$ --- consistent with flat, and far from the
Keplerian decline that would reach \hiRealKepler~km\,s$^{-1}$ at the outermost point. The two
estimators differ by a \emph{measured} \hiRealThrMinusEdge~km\,s$^{-1}$: the threshold crossing sits
on the profile wing, and the edge fit brings the level to \hiRealVflatEdge~km\,s$^{-1}$, within
$\hiRealExcessPctEdge$\% of the \citet{reid2019} $V_0$, so the threshold estimator's
$\hiRealExcessPct$\% excess is an estimator systematic measured on this data, not a cited one. An
inner-Galaxy flat curve is not, by itself, a standalone dark-matter detection. This is a tooling and
reproducibility contribution.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
That spiral-galaxy rotation curves stay flat to large radii, rather than declining with the visible
mass, is one of the cornerstones of the dark-matter case. For our own Galaxy the inner rotation curve
is measured from the HI~21\,cm line by the tangent-point (terminal-velocity) method
\citep{mccluregriffiths2007}: along an inner sightline the gas at the subcentral point moves fastest
along the line of sight, marking the high-velocity edge of the profile. We test whether this classic
measurement is reproducible from public survey data (each $(b,v)$ slice is a sub-megabyte file) with
a small, tested tool whose core estimator and geometry fit in a few dozen lines. This is a
validation, not a new measurement.

\section{Method}
\code{hi} is a pure-NumPy/astropy module (on the \code{jansky} helpers; \citealt{jansky}). For each
longitude $\ell$ it reads the LAB $(b,v)$ slice, takes the $b=0$ spectrum, and estimates the terminal
velocity two ways on the same spectrum: (i) the \emph{threshold} estimator, the most positive LSR
velocity with brightness temperature above 2~K (a conventional choice; sweeping the threshold over
1.5--5~K moves the flat mean only \hiRealSweepLoThr\ to \hiRealSweepHiThr~km\,s$^{-1}$, small
against the estimator systematic below); and (ii) the \emph{edge} estimator, an error-function fit
$T(v)=\frac{A}{2}\,\mathrm{erfc}\!\big((v-v_0)/\sqrt{2}w\big)+c$ to the profile's falling edge,
whose half-height velocity $v_0$ sits inside the edge rather than out on its wing, in the spirit of
the edge fitting of \citet{mccluregriffiths2016}. In every LAB spectrum used here the emission above
2~K is a single contiguous velocity run, so the threshold crossing is the top edge of the main
emission, not an isolated wing. The tangent point then gives
\begin{equation}
R = R_0\sin\ell, \qquad V(R) = v_\mathrm{term} + V_0\sin\ell,
\end{equation}
with $R_0=\hiRzero$~kpc and $V_0=\hiVzero$~km\,s$^{-1}$ from \citet{reid2019} (their best-fit solar
circular speed, not the older IAU 220~km\,s$^{-1}$). LAB velocities are tabulated in the kinematic
LSR frame, which uses the standard solar motion; the \citet{reid2019} constants are tied to their
fitted solar motion, and the residual frame mismatch injects a few km\,s$^{-1}$ at $\sim\sin\ell$,
small against the estimator terms tracked below. A synthetic $(\ell,v)$ slice with a known injected
flat curve and a realistic ($\sim$5~km\,s$^{-1}$) edge width drives the offline tests: the fixture
reproduces the threshold estimator's wing overshoot (\hiSynThrMinusEdge~km\,s$^{-1}$ against
\hiRealThrMinusEdge\ measured on LAB) while the edge estimator recovers the injected curve.

\section{Data}
We use the LAB survey \citep{kalberla2005}, the all-sky combination of the Leiden/Dwingeloo and
Instituto Argentino de Radioastronom\'ia surveys with stray-radiation correction, retrieved as
per-longitude $(b,v)$ FITS slices from VizieR. We sample \hiNlong\ longitudes from $\ell=10\arcdeg$ to
$80\arcdeg$ in the first quadrant and read the mid-plane ($b=0$) spectrum of each.

\section{Results}
Table~\ref{tab:curve} lists every point under both estimators; the recovered curve rises gently
across the inner disk and then flattens. Beyond the bar-dominated region ($R\lesssim\hiRealRmin$~kpc,
dropped), the \hiRealNflat\ points from $R\approx\hiRealRmin$ to $\hiRealRmax$~kpc give
$V=\hiRealVflat$~km\,s$^{-1}$ on the threshold estimator, where the point-to-point scatter is
\hiRealVflatScatter~km\,s$^{-1}$ and the standard error of the mean \hiRealVflatSem\
(Figure~\ref{fig:rc}). Flatness is quantified, not eyeballed: a least-squares slope over the flat
sample gives $\hiRealSlope\pm\hiRealSlopeErr$~km\,s$^{-1}$\,kpc$^{-1}$, consistent with zero, while
a Keplerian $V\propto R^{-1/2}$ normalised at the innermost flat point would fall to
\hiRealKepler~km\,s$^{-1}$ at the outermost --- the measured value stays $\sim$90~km\,s$^{-1}$
above it. The level is insensitive to the hand-chosen analysis parameters: sweeping the threshold
1.5--5~K moves the mean from \hiRealSweepLoThr\ to \hiRealSweepHiThr, dropping the innermost flat
point (which sits 0.07~kpc above the $R>\hiRealRmin$~kpc cut) gives \hiRealDropInner, and dropping
the two outermost points (the longitudes where local emission blends most with the terminal edge)
gives \hiRealDropOuterTwo~km\,s$^{-1}$. All variants are committed with the results.

\section{Limitations}
\paragraph{The normalization is an estimator statement, measured here.} The threshold curve sits
$\hiRealExcessPct$\% above $V_0=\hiVzero$; the edge fit, run on the same spectra, sits at
\hiRealVflatEdge~km\,s$^{-1}$ ($\hiRealExcessPctEdge$\%). The difference,
\hiRealThrMinusEdge~km\,s$^{-1}$ on the flat sample, is the wing overshoot of a threshold crossing
on a finite-width edge, reproduced quantitatively by the synthetic fixture
(\hiSynThrMinusEdge~km\,s$^{-1}$ at a 5~km\,s$^{-1}$ edge width). Note this is measured
in-house rather than imported: the $\sim$7~km\,s$^{-1}$ offset in \citet{mccluregriffiths2016} is
between the \citet{clemens1985} CO terminal curve and their fitted HI curve --- a cross-tracer,
cross-survey comparison they attribute only partly to the threshold method --- and is not a
substitute for the comparison done here. The residual $\sim$$\hiRealExcessPctEdge$\% under the edge
estimator is within the streaming-motion and frame-convention terms below. The quoted
\hiRealVflatScatter~km\,s$^{-1}$ is point-to-point scatter, orthogonal to this shared systematic:
an estimator bias moves all \hiRealNflat\ points together and leaves the scatter unchanged.
\paragraph{Circular orbits assumed.} $V = v_\mathrm{term} + V_0\sin\ell$ assumes the tangent-point gas
is on a circular orbit; spiral-arm and bar-driven streaming biases individual points and cannot be
removed without a model.
\paragraph{Bar region excluded.} Points at $R\lesssim\hiRealRmin$~kpc are dominated by the bar's
non-circular motions and are dropped from the mean.
\paragraph{Not, by itself, a dark-matter detection.} An inner flat curve is consistent with a
dark halo but can also arise from the disk's own mass; the unambiguous case needs the curve beyond
$R\approx R_0$, where the visible-mass contribution falls, a regime requiring a different method
and not covered here. The result is also first-quadrant, $b=0$ only, and the tangent-point method
applies solely for $R<R_0$.

\section{Discussion and reproducibility}
This is a validation: a small, tested, offline-capable tool recovers the approximately flat
inner Milky Way rotation curve from public LAB HI data, with the flatness quantified by a slope
fit, the Keplerian alternative excluded by $\sim$90~km\,s$^{-1}$ at the outermost point, and the
normalization traced to a measured estimator systematic rather than a citation. The analysis
regenerates from a clean checkout, and every number traces to the pipeline-written macros and
table \code{\textbackslash input}ted at the top of the source.
