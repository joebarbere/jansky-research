\begin{abstract}
The Jodrell Bank glitch catalogue \citep{espinoza2011,basu2022,jbocat} held
\gpRealNGlitchesRaw\ glitches from \gpRealNPulsarsRaw\ pulsars in the snapshot analysed here
(retrieved \gpRealRetrieved, committed with the results); excluding magnetars,
\gpRealNGlitches\ glitches from \gpRealNPulsars\ pulsars enter the analysis. The last population-statistics paper
\citep{basu2022} stops at end-2018; measured against that epoch in this snapshot,
\gpRealNPostSplit\ glitches post-date it (smaller than the naive difference of catalogue
totals, because \gpRealNRetroPre\ pre-2019 glitches are themselves retroactive additions
flagged new in the table). That paper characterised glitch size, not
per-pulsar waiting-time behaviour.
\citet{howitt2018} did classify per-pulsar waiting times (Poisson vs quasi-periodic), but only
for the few most-glitching pulsars on a pre-2018 sample and without gap-robustness. We extend that classification to
the full $\ge5$-glitch sample, add monitoring-gap robustness, and report the change
across the post-2018 data.

We classify each pulsar's inter-glitch waiting-time distribution as exponential (memoryless
Poisson, the null), quasi-periodic (regular, Vela/J0537-like), or clustered, using a
coefficient of variation calibrated against the exponential null by parametric bootstrap. The
load-bearing step is monitoring-gap excision: the archival
catalogue conflates real inter-glitch intervals with intervals in which a pulsar kept glitching
unobserved, and a single such gap misclassifies even the textbook quasi-periodic glitcher
J0537$-$6910 (2264-day RXTE$\to$NICER gap, raw coefficient of variation $\sim$2). After excising
waits far longer than a pulsar's median, the classifier recovers both known
quasi-periodic glitchers (J0537$-$6910 and Vela) as quasi-periodic (\gpRealKnownQpOK); a
synthetic recover-a-known separates injected exponential and quasi-periodic processes at a
false-positive rate of \gpSynFP.

Of \gpRealNGeCut\ pulsars above the count cut (magnetars excluded;
\gpRealNDropped\ further pulsar is silently removed when gap excision leaves too few intervals,
a second cut now stated), \gpRealNFit\ are classifiable: \gpRealNQp\ quasi-periodic,
\gpRealNExp\ exponential, \gpRealNClustered\ clustered. The aggregate quasi-periodic excess is
significant under both the nominal per-test rate (\gpRealExpectedFalseQp\ expected false,
binomial $p=\gpRealQpBinomP$) and the empirically measured count-dependent false rates
(Poisson-binomial $p=\gpRealQpPoisBinomP$); individual borderline members are not secure. The
single clustered pulsar is consistent with the all-Poisson chance expectation (binomial
$p=\gpRealClusteredBinomP$), and long-gap clustering is additionally suppressed by the gap
excision itself, so we make no clustering claim either way, deferring to
\citet{zhuzheng2025}. Adding the post-2018 glitches flips no classification: two apparent flips
in an earlier version of this analysis dissolved when the bootstrap was deepened from $4\times
10^3$ to $2\times10^5$ draws with per-pulsar null seeds (their margins were smaller than the
Monte-Carlo error of the decision), and \gpRealNNewly\ pulsars are newly classifiable. The
gap-robust classification, its measured selection function, and the finding that no
classification change survives the catalogue increment at current sample sizes are the
contribution, extending \citet{howitt2018} to the full sample.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A pulsar glitch is a sudden spin-up of a neutron star, the observable of an internal
angular-momentum transfer. The statistics of glitches (how large they are and how they are
spaced in time) constrain the interior physics. Two behaviours are established: most pulsars
glitch like a memoryless Poisson process (exponential waiting times; \citealt{howitt2018}), while a
few, notably Vela and PSR~J0537$-$6910, glitch quasi-periodically, J0537 roughly every 100 days
with a strong size--waiting-time correlation \citep{middleditch2006}.

The Jodrell Bank glitch catalogue \citep{espinoza2011,basu2022} is the standard population resource.
Its last population-statistics paper \citep{basu2022} ends at 2018 and models the aggregate
glitch-size and spin-down-change distributions with a two-component Gaussian mixture; it does not
perform a per-pulsar waiting-time model selection. \citet{howitt2018} did estimate per-pulsar
waiting-time distributions and separate Poisson-like from quasi-periodic glitchers, but for only the
few most-glitching pulsars on a pre-2018 sample and without correcting for monitoring gaps. Measured in the
snapshot analysed here, \gpRealNPostSplit\ glitches post-date that epoch (the naive difference
of catalogue totals, $\sim$184, double-counts \gpRealNRetroPre\ retroactively added pre-2019
entries). We extend the per-pulsar waiting-time classification to the full $\ge5$-glitch
sample, make it robust to monitoring gaps, and report how the classifications change when the
post-2018 data are added. The concurrent work of \citet{zhuzheng2025}
draws on the same sample but studies glitch-cluster period versus characteristic age, a
different quantity we do not overlap.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
We analyse a committed snapshot of the catalogue (retrieved \gpRealRetrieved; the parsed
table ships with the results, since three earlier retrievals returned three different pulsar
counts), group by J-name, and take successive-epoch differences as inter-glitch waiting times
(in years) for each pulsar with $\ge5$ glitches.

The archival waiting times mix real intervals with monitoring gaps.
A regular glitcher observed through a long hiatus records one enormous interval that is not physical;
J0537$-$6910's 2264-day RXTE$\to$NICER gap among $\sim$100-day intervals is the extreme case, and it
alone lifts its raw coefficient of variation to $\sim$2, so a naive fit calls it exponential. We drop
waits longer than six times a pulsar's median as monitoring gaps before classifying. For a regular
glitcher such a wait is almost certainly unobserved; for a genuine exponential only the far tail is
lost ($P(w>6\,\mathrm{median})\approx1.5\%$), so the null is barely biased. The unavoidable cost is
that a genuinely clustered pulsar (bursts of glitches separated by real long quiescent gaps)
has those gaps excised too, so long-gap clustering is under-detected; we state this plainly.

On the excised waits the coefficient of variation $\mathrm{cv}=\sigma/\mu$
is the regularity measure ($\sim$1 for exponential, $<$1 for quasi-periodic, $>$1 for clustered). We
calibrate it against the exponential null with a parametric bootstrap (the cv distribution of $n$
exponential waits; cv is scale-free), and classify quasi-periodic if the observed cv is
significantly low ($p<0.05$), clustered if significantly high, else exponential. The bootstrap null is
drawn with the same gap excision applied to it, so null and data undergo identical processing.
The bootstrap uses $2\times10^5$ draws with a per-pulsar seed derived from the J-name, so a
borderline decision cannot ride on a couple of shared null replicates (at the earlier
$4\times10^3$ draws the census's one reported flip had a margin of two replicates, and it
dissolved when the bootstrap was deepened), and every p-value is reported with its Monte-Carlo
standard error. This test replaced an initial gamma--exponential BIC that underweighted Vela's
mild-but-real regularity, so the statistic was, in part, reselected against the known objects.
Three validations are independent of that selection: the synthetic injection surface below; the
committed gap-factor sweep (quasi-periodic count 8--10 across thresholds 3--10$\times$, with the
single clustered call vanishing below 6$\times$); and one out-of-sample real anchor:
\citet{howitt2018} independently reclassified B1338$-$62 as quasi-periodic, and this census
reproduces that (cv 0.555) without ever having used the object in method selection. We exclude
magnetars/AXPs (\gpRealNMagnetars\ dropped), whose catalogued glitches are
X-ray-outburst-driven rather than rotation-powered.

We reclassify each pulsar on the pre-2019 (Basu) subset and on the full
catalogue, and report the post-2018 delta: which cross the $\ge5$-glitch threshold only with the new data
(``newly classifiable'') and which had a real class in both epochs but changed it (``flipped'').

\section{Synthetic recover-a-known}
The classifier's selection function is measured as a surface over injected regularity and glitch
count, not a scalar. An earlier version of this validation injected quasi-periodic series only
at cv 0.12, four to five times more regular than every real detection (committed cv range
0.18--0.62), and returned completeness exactly 1.0 at every grid point, a test that could not
fail. The surface can: completeness is 1.0 for cv $\le$ 0.3 at 10 or more glitches, but falls
to 0.70 at (cv 0.5, $n=10$), 0.44 at (cv 0.5, $n=6$) and 0.16 at (cv 0.7, $n=6$): the regime
where several of the real detections and every borderline call live, so the census's
quasi-periodic count is a floor with respect to mildly regular small-$n$ pulsars. Injected
Poisson series disaggregate the old pooled ``false-positive rate'' (\gpSynFP, which mixed three
different outcomes) into what actually happens: called quasi-periodic at 0.04--0.12 depending on
count, called clustered at 0.04--0.10, and dropped entirely by excision at up to 0.02. The
count-dependent false-quasi-periodic rates feed the population significance below. A
quasi-periodic series with an injected monitoring gap is misclassified without excision and
correctly recovered with it, the J0537 scenario in miniature. Clustered processes are
recoverable only with excision disabled, as expected.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
The real-data recover-a-known passes: both established quasi-periodic glitchers come out
quasi-periodic, J0537$-$6910 (cv~0.53 after excising its one monitoring gap) and Vela
(cv~0.57), so \gpRealKnownQpOK. This required the gap excision: without it J0537 is misclassified
exponential, which is itself the paper's methodological point.

The census: \gpRealNGeCut\ pulsars have $\ge5$ glitches; \gpRealNDropped\ of them is
removed by a second, previously unstated cut (gap excision leaving too few intervals, a
removal that acts preferentially on long-gap, clustered-candidate pulsars), and the
\gpRealNFit\ classifiable are \gpRealNExp\ exponential, \gpRealNQp\ ($\sim$26\%)
quasi-periodic, and \gpRealNClustered\ clustered. The aggregate quasi-periodic excess is
significant against the nominal per-test rate (\gpRealExpectedFalseQp\ expected false, binomial
$p=\gpRealQpBinomP$) and remains so against the empirically measured count-dependent false
rates (\gpRealExpectedFalseQpEmp\ expected, Poisson-binomial $p=\gpRealQpPoisBinomP$);
individual borderline members are not secure. The exponential majority matches the
Poisson-process picture of \citet{howitt2018}, whose independent reclassification of
B1338$-$62 this census reproduces out-of-sample; the quasi-periodic minority is the natural set
in which to look for reservoir/threshold behaviour. The single clustered source (B2224$+$65, on
four retained intervals at $p=0.039$) is consistent with the all-Poisson chance expectation
(\gpRealExpectedFalseClustered\ expected false, binomial $p=\gpRealClusteredBinomP$), and gap
excision additionally suppresses this class by construction, so no clustering claim is made in
either direction.

One diagnostic surfaced by this revision deserves its own statement: the census's per-pulsar
$p$-values are not uniform under the exponential null (mean \gpRealMeanPRegular\ against 0.5,
KS $p=10^{-4}$). Either the population is mildly more regular than Poisson well beyond the 8
individually significant members, or the bootstrap null is too dispersed for the real
monitoring process (a minimum resolvable glitch separation truncates short waits and lowers
observed cv). Both readings are consistent with the aggregate excess; distinguishing them needs
a monitoring-aware null, which we leave stated as the principal open methodological question.

The post-2018 increment: the \gpRealNPostSplit\ measured post-2018 glitches flip
\gpRealNFlipped\ classifications among the \gpRealNStable\ pulsars classifiable in both
epochs, and make \gpRealNNewly\ pulsars newly classifiable. An earlier version of this
analysis reported one flip (J2229$+$6114); at $4\times10^3$ bootstrap draws its margin was two
null replicates, and at $2\times10^5$ draws with per-pulsar seeds it dissolves, along with a
second borderline quasi-periodic call (B1758$-$23). The honest statement is that no
classification change is secure at current per-pulsar sample sizes: with $\le$10 waits a
single new glitch moves a borderline call, exactly the regime where more monitoring, not new
physics, is what matters.

Four limitations bound what this classification can say. Glitch detection is incomplete and
uneven across the monitoring history, so
waiting-time regularity is a lower bound; the $\ge5$-glitch cut selects the most active glitchers; the
gap excision assumes large waits are monitoring gaps, which suppresses genuine long-gap clustering;
and the flips are single-glitch-sensitive. None of these is hidden: the recover-a-known,
false-positive rate, and count-dependent accuracy bound what the classification can and cannot say.
