\title{The Steep Radio Spectra of Pulsars, Reproduced from the ATNF Catalogue, and the
Millisecond--Normal Comparison}

\author[0009-0008-3289-4447]{Joseph Barbere}
\affiliation{Independent researcher}

\begin{abstract}
Pulsars are among the steepest-spectrum radio sources: their flux density falls as
$S_\nu\propto\nu^\alpha$ with a mean two-frequency index near $-1.8$, far steeper than the $\sim-0.7$
of ordinary synchrotron sources (Maron et al.\ 2000; Bates et al.\ 2013; Jankowski et al.\ 2018). We
reproduce this from the public ATNF Pulsar Catalogue \citep{atnf} with a small, tested, CPU-only tool,
forming the 400$\to$1400~MHz spectral index from the catalogue's tabulated S400 and S1400 flux
densities. For the \psrN\ pulsars with a tabulated flux at both frequencies, the mean index is
$\langle\alpha\rangle=\psrMeanAlpha\pm\psrMeanAlphaSE$ (median \psrMedianAlpha, scatter
\psrStdAlpha) --- at the steep end of the published $-1.4$ to $-1.8$ range, with the statistical
error far below the flux-scale systematics. The selection is coverage-dominated rather than purely
flux-limited:
only \psrNSfourHundred\ of \psrNcatalogue\ pulsars have any published 400-MHz flux (against
\psrNSfourteenHundred\ at 1400 MHz), and above the completeness limit where the joint-flux
requirement can no longer remove steep sources the mean steepens to \psrCompleteMean. Splitting at
the standard $P<30$~ms boundary, the \psrNmsp\ millisecond pulsars have mean
$\alpha=\psrMeanAlphaMsp$ versus $\psrMeanAlphaNormal$ for the \psrNnormal\ normal pulsars, a
difference of $\psrAlphaDiff\pm\psrAlphaDiffSE$ ($\psrAlphaDiffSigma\sigma$; permutation
$p=\psrPermP$). What the data exclude is the complement of the 95\% interval on that difference,
[$\psrCIlo$, $\psrCIhi$] in $\alpha$; the conventional $2\sigma$ threshold \psrAlphaResolvable\ is
the offset detected with only 50\% power, and 90\% power needs \psrNinetyPower. All are consistent
with \citet{kramer1998} and \citet{jankowski2018}. This is a reproducibility and tooling
contribution, not a new result.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A pulsar's radio spectrum is one of its defining bulk properties, and pulsars are steep-spectrum
sources: surveys recover far more of them at low frequencies, and the population mean
two-frequency index sits near $-1.6$ to $-1.8$ \citep{maron2000,bates2013,jankowski2018}. Whether
millisecond pulsars differ from the normal population has been studied repeatedly, with the consensus
being little or no difference \citep{kramer1998,jankowski2018}. We test the most reproducible version,
using the public ATNF catalogue and a $\sim$hundred-line tool to measure the population mean index
and to test whether millisecond pulsars are flatter.

\section{Methods}
\code{pulsarspec} is a pure-NumPy module (on the \code{jansky} helpers; \citealt{jansky}) that composes
the project's two-point spectral-index helper: for every catalogued pulsar with both an S400 and an
S1400 flux density it computes $\alpha=\ln(S_{1400}/S_{400})/\ln(1400/400)$ and classifies it as
millisecond ($P<30$~ms) or normal. Every analysis function is tested against a synthetic pulsar
population with injected steep spectra and a flatter-millisecond sub-population, so the suite runs
offline; the network fetch itself is exercised only by the real run.

\section{Data}
The ATNF Pulsar Catalogue \citep{atnf} (VizieR \code{B/psr}) tabulates heterogeneous literature flux
densities S400 (400~MHz) and S1400 (1400~MHz) in mJy and the spin period $P$. \code{B/psr} is a
frozen snapshot (published 2017-07-18) behind the live \code{psrcat}, so the counts here are
epoch-specific. An absent flux is \emph{no published measurement at that frequency}, not a
non-detection: of the \psrNcatalogue\ catalogued pulsars, \psrNSfourHundred\ have any S400 and
\psrNSfourteenHundred\ any S1400 --- the binding constraint is 400-MHz coverage, since the modern
surveys that discovered most of the population did not observe there. \psrN\ have positive fluxes
at both frequencies and enter the spectral-index sample (\psrNrejected\ further row, J0540$-$6919,
tabulates both but records $S_{400}=0$ and is dropped by the positivity requirement). Per arm, the
joint sample keeps \psrNmsp\ of \psrNmspCat\ catalogued millisecond pulsars and \psrNnormal\ of
\psrNnormalCat\ normal ones --- the millisecond arm is the more heavily truncated. The analysed
rows are committed as \code{results/pulsarspec\_sources.csv}.

\section{Results}
The \psrN\ pulsars have a mean spectral index $\langle\alpha\rangle=\psrMeanAlpha\pm\psrMeanAlphaSE$
(statistical), median \psrMedianAlpha, with a broad scatter (\psrStdAlpha; Figure~\ref{fig:spectra},
left) --- consistent with the steep-spectrum results of \citet{bates2013} and \citet{jankowski2018},
at the steep end of their $-1.4$ to $-1.8$ range. The joint-flux selection biases this number flat:
above $S_{400}\geq\psrCompleteCut$~mJy --- the flux at which no source with $\alpha\geq-3$ can have
been lost to the sample's own effective S1400 floor (\psrCompleteFloor~mJy, its 1st percentile) ---
the \psrCompleteN-source completeness-limited mean is \psrCompleteMean\ (median
\psrCompleteMedian), so the truncation is worth $\sim$0.1 in $\alpha$ and the honest headline is
``\psrMeanAlpha\ raw, \psrCompleteMean\ above the completeness limit.'' (Raising the \emph{S1400}
floor instead is not a clean check: it conditions on the numerator of $\alpha$ and flattens the
mean mechanically.) Splitting the sample, the \psrNmsp\ millisecond pulsars have mean
$\alpha=\psrMeanAlphaMsp$ (scatter \psrStdAlphaMsp) against $\psrMeanAlphaNormal$ (scatter
\psrStdAlphaNormal) for the \psrNnormal\ normal pulsars. The difference (millisecond minus normal,
i.e.\ millisecond pulsars marginally \emph{flatter}) is $\psrAlphaDiff\pm\psrAlphaDiffSE$, or
$\psrAlphaDiffSigma\sigma$ (two-sided permutation $p=\psrPermP$; the median difference is
\psrMedianDiff), where the error is dominated by the millisecond arm. What the comparison
yields is a bound: the data exclude offsets outside [$\psrCIlo$, $\psrCIhi$] (95\% interval
on the difference). The conventional $2\sigma$ threshold \psrAlphaResolvable\ is the offset this
pair of subsamples detects with 50\% power; 90\% power needs \psrNinetyPower. A genuinely flatter
millisecond population with a small offset would be indistinguishable from none here. The verdict
survives the one free classification constant: sweeping the millisecond boundary over 10--100~ms
(committed) flips the sign of the difference and never approaches its own $2\sigma$. The
$\alpha$--period plane (Figure~\ref{fig:spectra}, right) shows no strong trend across three decades
in period.

\section{Discussion and reproducibility}
The steep mean pulsar spectrum and the absence of a resolvable millisecond--normal difference are
both recovered from public data with a small tested tool. The contribution is reproducibility and
tooling (a validated pulsar spectral-index statistic over the both-band subset, with its own
selection function quantified), not a new result. The limitations are intrinsic to a two-frequency
index from a heterogeneous catalogue. (i) It misses the spectral curvature and low-frequency
turnovers that multi-frequency fits resolve \citep{jankowski2018}. (ii) The selection is
coverage-dominated with a flux-limit component: most of the catalogue simply has no published
400-MHz flux, so the sample skews toward older, brighter, low-frequency-discovered pulsars ---
a selection whose net direction is not obvious --- while the flux-limit component is measured
above (the completeness cut steepens the mean by $\sim$0.1). The per-arm coverage also differs
(millisecond pulsars are the more truncated arm), so the bound applies to the observed subsets,
not the intrinsic populations. (iii) The errors are statistical floors: the broad scatter reflects
the $\sim$tens-of-percent catalogue flux-scale and scintillation uncertainties as much as
intrinsic spread, and a flux-scale offset $f$ at one band maps to
$\Delta\alpha=\ln(1+f)/\ln 3.5$ --- 0.076 at 10\%, 0.146 at 20\%. A scale error common to both arms
cancels in the millisecond--normal difference but shifts the headline mean by exactly that amount;
one that differs between the source literatures feeding the two arms does not cancel, and at
$\sim$30\% it is the size of the quoted $2\sigma$ resolution. The Welch error also assumes
independent per-source fluxes, which publication clustering violates in the optimistic direction.
Every number above is written by the pipeline into macros that are \code{\textbackslash input}
directly, and the analysis regenerates from a clean checkout on public data.
